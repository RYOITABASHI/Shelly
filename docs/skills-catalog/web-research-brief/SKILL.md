---
name: web-research-brief
description: Turn a browser-pane research session into a short, cited brief instead of handing back a pile of open tabs and raw page text.
---
When a task involves researching a topic via the browser pane and reporting
back, structure the work so the output is usable without the reader having
to re-do the research themselves:

1. **State the question you're actually answering** at the top, in your own
   words — a research brief that starts with findings but never states the
   question forces the reader to reverse-engineer what was being asked.
2. **Read enough sources to cross-check, not just the first hit.** A single
   page (especially a forum post, blog, or SEO-optimized summary page) can
   be wrong or outdated; corroborate a load-bearing claim against at least
   one more independent source before stating it as fact.
3. **Cite what you used.** Each non-obvious claim gets a source (title +
   URL), so the reader can verify or dig deeper — don't present synthesized
   conclusions as if they came from nowhere.
4. **Separate fact from inference.** Say plainly when you're stating what a
   source said versus when you're drawing a conclusion the sources didn't
   state directly — mixing the two erodes trust in both.
5. **Note what you couldn't confirm**, rather than silently dropping it —
   a stale page, a paywall, or conflicting sources are useful information
   in themselves ("sources disagree on X; A says ___, B says ___").
6. **End with a direct answer**, not just a list of findings — if the
   original question was "should we do X," say what you'd conclude from the
   research, clearly separated from the supporting evidence above it.
